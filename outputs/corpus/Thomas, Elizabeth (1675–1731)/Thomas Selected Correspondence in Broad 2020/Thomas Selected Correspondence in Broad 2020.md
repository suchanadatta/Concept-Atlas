# 2 Elizabeth Thomas (1675-1731)

Today Elizabeth Thomas, or 'Corinna,' is better known as a poet than a philosopher. During the early eighteenth century, she was an active participant in manuscript circulation networks in England, and in 1722 she published an anonymous collection titled Miscellany Poems on Several Subjects. $^{1}$ She was also the co-author of Pylades and Corinna, first published posthumously in 1731, and a second volume, titled The Honourable Lovers, published in 1732. $^{2}$ These companion volumes contain a memoir, letters, poetry, and other materials pertaining to Thomas's courtship with Richard Gwinnett (1675-1717), a gentleman playwright of Gloucestershire. $^{3}$ Although Thomas never wrote a philosophical treatise of her own, her writings demonstrate an interest in numerous philosophical figures and ideas of the period. In addition to the poems 'On the Stoick Philosophy' and 'To Sir Roger L'Estrange, on his Seneca', she wrote pieces dedicated to the philosopher-theologian John Norris and the feminist thinkers Mary Astell and Mary Chudleigh; $^{4}$ she also produced an abstract of Isaac Newton's Opticks (1704). $^{5}$ This chapter provides selections from her correspondences with Norris (2.1.1-9) and Chudleigh (2.2.1-3), as well as her 'Philosophical Letters, concerning the true Nature of Love $^{6}$ with Captain Richard Hemington, also known as 'the Stoic' (2.3.1-5). At the start of the eighteenth century, John Norris had earnt a reputation among women as someone who was 'not so narrow-Souled as to confine Learning to his own Sex' (1.1.1). $^{7}$ Inspired by this public persona, Elizabeth Thomas wrote to Norris in about 1699, to send him a reverential 'Ode' on his writings, applauding him for his 'Instructive Ethics' and for helping others to cultivate a 'seraphic' rather than 'sensual' love of beauty (2.1.1). Norris responded by urging Thomas to forsake poetry in favour of 'serious and usefull studies' (2.1.2), and Thomas wrote back to request his intellectual guidance (see 2.1.3). On 12 July 1699, Norris then sent Thomas a long reading list almost completely made up of Cartesian philosophical treatises and works by Anglican theologians, the Port Royal school, and the Cambridge Platonist Henry More (2.1.4). In many respects, this list provides Thomas with a training ground in Norris's own moral, theological, and metaphysical systems. He recommends not only Descartes's Meditations on First Philosophy (1641) and Principles of Philosophy (1644), Arnauld and Nicole's Logic, or the Art of Thinking (1662), and other popularizations of Cartesian ideas, but also the chief works of the unorthodox Cartesian Nicolas Malebranche, his Search After Truth (1674-5) and Dialogues on Metaphysics and Religion (1688). In his own philosophy, Norris upholds an orthodox Cartesian position to the extent that he affirms the existence of two distinct substances, the immaterial soul and the material body, and regards human persons as consisting in a substantial union of these two things. Like Descartes, he also maintains that all the behaviour of nonhuman animals can be explained in terms of the mechanistic principles governing matter in motion alone; there is no need to posit the existence of animal souls. But Norris radically departs from Descartes when it comes to his theory of causation. Descartes maintains that bodies are capable of causally interacting with minds, and minds are capable of affecting bodies. But Norris maintains that God is the only true causal agent in the created world, and that material things are completely incapable of having any causal influence on the im-material mind, and vice versa. You know very well, you can have an Idea of a far greater Perfection than you are like to meet out of your self; and yet I believe you'd think your self happier than any of your Neighbours, could you find one some Degrees below it; and would, I doubt not, in Justice give him as great a Share in your Affections, as by his approaching Perfection he is capable of. This is the Language of Truth (Madam) which I can venture to speak only to you, and one more in the World; for all that I have ever read on this Subject deny, that a Friendship can be between more than two. I wonder at none so much as Mr. Norris, who 'tis probable inclined you to this Opinion, which 'tis impossible you can have any regular Idea of. I shall request my Lady's $^{165}$ Arbitration when she comes to Town, which I hope will be very soon, and am highly delighted with your making Choice of so good a Judge. I am, with Truth and Respect,

MADAM,

Your most obedient Servant, etc.
