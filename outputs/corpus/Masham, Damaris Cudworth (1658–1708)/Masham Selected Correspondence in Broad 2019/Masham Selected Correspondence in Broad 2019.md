# Damaris Cudworth Masham (1659-1708)

Damaris Masham (née Cudworth) was a moral philosopher who published two short treatises—A Discourse Concerning the Love of God (1696) and Occasional Thoughts in Reference to a Vertuous or Christian Life (1705) both of which bear the marks of John Locke's influence. $^{1}$ The following correspondence includes twenty-nine out of forty-five surviving letters between Masham and Locke from 1682 to 1688. There is also a selection of letters from Masham to the Genevan philosopher-theologian Jean Le Clerc and to the German philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, from the first few years of the eighteenth century. Taken as a whole, this correspondence reveals that while Masham constantly espoused a practicable ethical approach (a 'lived' Christianity), she also adapted and revised the epistemological foundations of her views. More specifically, in her letters it is possible to see a decisive shift away from an early adherence to the Platonist epistemology of her father Ralph Cudworth and his Cambridge colleagues John Smith and Henry More, toward a later commitment to Locke's empiricist theory of knowledge.

In her early letters to Locke, Masham defends certain Platonist views about divine intervention in the search for knowledge (3.1.2-7). In the first few months of their acquaintance, Locke and Masham engaged in a friendly epistolary quarrel about a book they had both been reading, the Cambridge Platonist John Smith's Select Discourses of 1660. They specifically discuss Smith's first discourse, 'A Præfatory Discourse concerning The true Way or Method of attaining to Divine Knowledge'. The main point of their dispute concerns whether or not Smith is an enthusiast, someone who bases knowledge of religious truths upon fancy and imagination alone, rather than reason. Locke affirms that Smith is an enthusiast; Masham denies it.

It is simply the case that God reveals to him certain truths—truths that are compatible with reason once the sage has obtained a higher spiritual disposition to receive them.

In her later published writings and in her letters to Leibniz (1704-5),however, Masham evinces a stronger commitment to Lockean empiricism. In her 1705 published work Occasional Thoughts, Masham explicitly asserts that human knowledge consists in the perception of the agreement or disagreement of ideas derived from sensory experience and reflection on the operations of the mind. Here there is no caveat that the highest form of knowledge requires direct supernatural assistance in addition to natural reason. Rather, in defining the parameters of knowledge, Masham considers only 'what light we receive from Nature to direct our actions'. Following Locke (Essay IV.xvii.2), she regards reason as the faculty that enables human beings to discover the agreement or disagreement of ideas, and to determine their assent accordingly. These same empiricist principles seemingly lie behind Masham's critique of Leibniz's notion of 'forms' in their 1704 correspondence. In one letter (3.3.2), Masham asks Leibniz to clarify what he means by 'forms' in his philosophy, since she cannot form a clear idea of the concept. Leibniz sometimes refers to them as 'primitive forces' or ' souls', and then also as 'constitutive of substances'; yet he denies that they are either matter or spirit. In response to Masham's request, Leibniz explains that his forms are the basic constituents of things: those unextended simple beings or ' atoms of substances' that have force as their essence (3.3.3). But still Masham insists that she has no 'positive idea' of the essence of these substances; she is simply unable 'to conceive an existence of that which is No Where' (3.3.4). In his Essay, Locke, too, maintains that we have no distinct idea of the real essences of what are commonly known as 'forms' (II.xxxi.6). While Masham gradually revises and refines her epistemological outlook, she never deviates from her commitment to human sociability as the basis of a happy life. A constant theme of her writings is the need to find an even balance between human sociability and a suitable detachment from worldly concerns, in order to obtain happiness. Her early letters to Locke contain a continuous dialectic in which she attempts to reconcile these two opposing viewpoints: that is, a Christianized-Stoic philosophy of withdrawal from the external world, and a moral-religious ideal of human sociability and friendship with others. To live well, according to the ancient Stoics, moral agents must take control of their lives and determine their own life paths through the exercise of reason, and by desiring ends that are within their power to obtain. One of the upshots of the Stoic viewpoint is that virtue lies in the perfection of one's reason and that virtue is the only good that one should pursue unconditionally, for its own sake. Society and friendship are neither good nor bad, but rather 'indifferents'. Masham expresses this sentiment when she says that 'Nothing in this World deserves Half the Care we usually Bestow upon it' and 'Happiness is not to be looked for in any thing without us' (3.1.14).

Nevertheless, Masham consistently comes back to the view that, while worldly detachment might be a laudable ideal, in practice human beings are constitutionally unable to live without loving and desiring their fellow creatures. ## 3.1 Damaris Cudworth Masham and John Locke

John Locke and Damaris Masham probably first met in late 1681 through their mutual friend Edward Clarke. Shortly thereafter, Locke found some pretext to send Masham a letter. Thus began a correspondence that continued for many years, throughout Locke's period of exile in Holland from 1683 to 1689. Upon his return to England, Locke met with Masham and her husband Sir Francis, who eventually persuaded Locke to take up residence with them in their country manor house at Oates in Essex. And since you touch upon the same subject in your letter, I would say to you, Madam, that in my opinion matter, even though it is devoid of consciousness, can act in a way that allows it to serve an end, without it needing to have a particular direction applied to it by God, or some other intelligence during the action; for one can conceive that God gave it first a structure which enables it to produce in time actions consistent with reason. Since our engineers, whose talents are so limited, can provide examples of this in certain circumstances, by means of machines that imitate reason, it is easy to infer that what is most beautiful in the artifices of man must be all the more so in the works of God, and that God, whose art infinitely surpasses our own, could obtain this effect in any kind of circumstance, without needing specific plastic natures or his own particular cooperation to the actual formation of animals. This is where my hypothesis differs from others. For I do not wish, with respect to natural things, to appeal to God's peculiar guidance, which could only be miraculous, nor have recourse to incorporeal plastic natures which would have no advantage over the machine. I would therefore say that bodies have in them plastic natures, but these natures are nothing other than their own machines, which produce excellent works without consciousness of what they do, because these Machines have been invented by a Master who is even more excellent. The plastic force is in the Machine, but the idea of what it does is in God. Hence, I deny any power to creatures whose possibility cannot be clearly conceived. And the formation of animals is all the more explicable, since it always comes from a preformation, that is to say, that there is always an animal of its form. Habitual actions too (like those that we perform in playing the harpsichord, without thinking about everything that we are doing) confirm what I have just said, namely that the Machine is capable of acting reasonably without knowing it, when it has been so predisposed by a rational substance. For one would not play so well without thinking enough about what one was doing, if one had not given oneself beforehand the disposition necessary for that, which is the result of having thought about it in the process of learning to play.
