# Elizabeth Berkeley Burnet (1661-1709)

Elizabeth Burnet (née Blake, former married name Berkeley) was the author of a popular religious manual titled A Method of Devotion (1708), reissued several times in the eighteenth century. This work was written during the period of Burnet's widowhood, from 1694 to 1700, following the death of her first husband Robert Berkeley, and prior to her second marriage to the prominent Whig theologian Gilbert Burnet, Bishop of Salisbury. Among Elizabeth Burnet's close friends during this period were two unlikely associates: the philosopher-theologian Edward Stillingfleet, Bishop of Worcester, and the philosopher John Locke. In the late 1690s, these men were engaged in a bitter ongoing dispute with each other. The following selection of Burnet's letters to Locke attest to the fact that she was a significant intermediary in this exchange. More than this, the themes of the correspondence suggest that Burnet may have developed some passages of her Method in response to this dispute.

The controversy began in 1697 when Stillingfleet published his Discourse in Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity, with a final chapter on the dangerous religious consequences of Locke's new empirical 'way of ideas'. $^{2}$ Stillingfleet acknowledged that Locke himself had never challenged the doctrine of the Trinity in his Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690), but the bishop highlighted the fact that other writers, such as the Irish freethinker John Toland, had followed through on the sceptical and irreligious implications of Locke's ideas. Stillingfleet was concerned that Locke's Essay provided philosophical grounding for heretical ideas, such as denial of the Trinity, denial of the soul's immateriality and immortality, and even denial of the existence of God.

Locke was deeply resentful of this unwanted publicity for his Essay. He was especially resentful of the fact that Stillingfleet had grouped him alongside the reviled Toland as one of 'the Gentlemen of the new way of reasoning' who had undermined the articles of the Christian faith. Stillingfleet's name carried considerable weight and authority in this period: as the author of several popular works, he was better known and more widely respected than Locke, a relative newcomer to the field. As a consequence, Locke was compelled to clear his name. His first response to Stillingfleet was A Letter To the Right Reverend Edward Lord Bishop of Worcester (1697), complaining that he had been unfairly vilified by association. He accused Stillingfleet of failing to read his work carefully and charitably, independently of Toland's views. Stillingfleet responded to Locke with his defiant Answer to Mr. Locke's Letter (dated March 1697), then Locke issued an equally defiant Reply (June 1697). Stillingfleet fought back with his second Answer (September 1697), and Locke issued a further 452-page Reply (May 1698). Finally, Stillingfleet died in 1699 and the dispute came to an end.

The following correspondence shows that in the middle of this controversy stood Elizabeth Burnet (then Berkeley), friend to both writers and deeply disturbed by the lack of charity being shown on both sides. In one of her first letters (4.1.2), she hints that Locke had become annoyed at some remarks that Stillingfleet had made in a personal letter to Burnet. It is likely that Stillingfleet's remarks were about Locke's first Letter in the debate—a possible catalyst for Locke's next Reply to Stillingfleet. Burnet tries to dispel Locke's annoyance by taking the blame on herself: she might have misread Stillingfleet's point, she suggests, and presented it in a bad light. She reassures Locke that Stillingfleet holds him in great esteem and respect, and pleads with Locke to be 'charitable' and 'just' toward his adversary.

Burnet's efforts were in vain. In late August or early September that year, Locke published his Reply to Stillingfleet and sent Burnet a bound presentation copy. At Locke's request, in her letter of 22 September 1697 (4.1.4), Burnet provides a lengthy 'reflection' on this work. Quite candidly, she tells Locke that it would be best for him to remain silent in future. He is too quick to take personal offence, he has been unfair and uncharitable to Stillingfleet, and his comments are often irrelevant and pernickety. She expresses a hope that he might use his philosophical skill to find points of agreement with Stillingfleet in future, and to show greater 'generosity and disinterestness' toward his opponent.

Burnet's reflections demonstrate that she is a philosophically astute reader of this dispute. In his Reply to Stillingfleet, Locke is at pains to emphasize that his way of reasoning differs from that of Toland: while Toland makes clear and distinct ideas a prerequisite for certainty, Locke asserts that certainty consists in the perception of the agreement or disagreement of ideas, whether or not those ideas are clear and distinct. In response, Burnet points out that Stillingfleet impugns not only Toland's way of certainty by clear and distinct ideas, but also Locke's way of certainty by the agreement and disagreement of ideas. Both criteria of certainty potentially lead to scepticism and irreligion, in the bishop's view. To settle the dispute, Burnet suggests, it is not enough for Locke simply to distinguish his position from that of Toland. Instead, Locke might emphasize to Stillingfleet that he (Locke) does not require certainty in order to assent to matters of faith. In his Essay (III.xviii.2), Locke asserts that faith consists simply in assent to propositions based on the credit of the proposer as coming from God (divine revelation), not on reason. From this viewpoint, lack of certainty in our ideas (of substance, person, essence, and so forth) does not necessarily threaten belief in the articles of the Christian faith.

Burnet also develops her own ideas as a result of this exchange: namely on the topic of the proper art of disputation. In her letters to Locke, Burnet emphasizes the importance of showing the character traits of charity, generosity, open-mindedness, disinterestedness, and control over the passions of anger and resentment, in the context of philosophical dispute. She accuses Locke of wilfully reading Stillingfleet in the worst possible light, and of being angry and resentful of Stillingfleet's criticisms. In one part, Locke belittles Stillingfleet for relying too much on authoritative sources. Locke says:

if I had been guilty of affecting to be thought an Original, a Correction could not have come from any Body so disinterested in the Case, as your Lordship; since your Lordship so much declines being thought an Original, for writing in a way, wherein it is hard to avoid thinking that you are the first, till some other can be produced that writ so before You. $^{3}$

Burnet's general argumentative strategy in her letters is to press Locke with principles that he already holds. In the opening programmatic statement of the Essay, Locke declares that his main objective is to help readers attain contentment and satisfaction with the knowledge that they have available to them in their current state (that is, as embodied human subjects). The problem that Locke seeks to address is that there are so many different, contradictory 'Perswasions' among men, embraced with equal 'Fondness and Devotion' and maintained with equal 'Resolution, and Eagerness'.4 Men strive to extend their inquiries beyond their human capacities and to seek for truth where it cannot be found. It is thus not surprising, he observes, that men 'Raise Questions' and 'multiply Disputes' without ever coming to 'any clear Resolution'. Their tempers serve only to continue and increase their doubts, and at last to confirm them 'in perfect Scepticism'.5 Locke aims to resolve these difficulties. In his view, disputes arise because men have misplaced passions or emotions about their beliefs: they feel an inordinate fondness, devotion, resolution, and eagerness about them. When they realise that they cannot adequately resolve their disputes, these feelings turn to hopelessness and despair. ## 4.1 Elizabeth Berkeley Burnet and John Locke

Burnet and Locke seem to have met sometime in the mid-1690s, when they became near neighbours in London. When Locke visited the city for short periods, he would stay at Robert Pawling's house in Little Lincoln's Inn Fields; and when Burnet (then Berkeley) came to town, she would reside with her sister Mary Dormer, also in Lincoln's Inn Fields. The following selection of letters reveal Burnet to be an active third party in the famous Locke-Stillingfleet dispute, once described as 'one of the most memorable controversies in the history of philosophy'. $^{14}$ Burnet comments on all three of Locke's responses to Stillingfleet from 1697 to 1698.
