LETTER I.

Bemerton, April 25. 1699.

MADAM,

YOU have here my particular Thanks for your very extraordinary and obliging Present, which entertained me with great Pleasure and Wonder, and in which I can find nothing to censure but the Meanness of the Subject, though to confess the Truth that is my Fault more than yours *. You have, I perceive, a very pregnant Genius for Poetry, and, I doubt not, for greater Things, and it you will be advised by one, who has had some Conversation with the Muses, you should not employ very much of your Time in their Company, which in you would be better bestowed upon more serious and useful Studies.

God has given you a great Force and Strength of Spirit, and it is Pity, but that its Motion should be as well determined. I pray him to direct it upon the best Things, and in the best Way, remaining with the greatest Respect,

Your very humble and obliged Servant,

J. NORRIS.

LETTER II.

GOOD MADAM,

I pray, forgive my Rudeness in deferring so long my Answer to your obliging Letter, of which some accidental Interruptions, together with my late Indispositions of Health, have been the Occasion. I should very gladly comply with your Request, in giving you such poor Assistance as I am able, towards the Direction of your Studies, but cannot so well do it at this Distance, and upon that little Knowledge I have of you, unless you will be pleased to let me know what your Education has hitherto been in the Way of Learning, what Progress you have made in it, what Languages you understand besides your own, that so I may know what Books you are capable of reading, and withal what your Design is, and what Sort of Books they are that you would read, whether such as serve only to the Promotion of Christian Life and Practice, or such as tend more immediately to the Illumination of your Mind, and the Improvement of your Reason and Understanding in the Way of Science and Speculation. If you please, Madam, to give me some few necessary Hints concerning these Things, that I may not be altogether in the dark in what I have to do, you shall receive my Instruction and Advice. In the mean time, you have the Prayers and best Wishes of

Bemerton, June 12. 1699.

Your humble Servant,

J. NORRIS.

LETTER V.

MADAM,

Need, and I beg your Pardon, for not answering your Letter (for which I thank you) so soon as I should, and indeed as I would, if I had not met with many Hindrances since I received it, which as I remember was no sooner than May Day, though it be dated the 30th of March. You cannot imagine what a Hurry my Life is, and what little Command I have of my Time, which instead of being free and intire for my own Use and the Service of my Friends, is lost and dispersed among a Company of Strangers, to whose importune Visits I am continually exposed, an Unhappiness, which I can more easily lament than remedy, but which my Friends I hope will forgive. As to the Objection, which the learned Person you speak of, is pleased to make against the Theory of the Ideal World, that it supposes Precogitancy in God; I grant it most readily, and 'tis what I contend for as a necessary Requisite to his Operations ad extra, or doing any thing without himself, which cannot be effected without some Preconception of it within, unless you will suppose God to act ignorantly, and without knowing what he does, or is about to do, which is impossible. But then I deny that all Precogitancy is an Imperfection. Indeed

## 104 Mr. Norris's Anfwer.

Precogitancy as it signifies a laborious and anxious Fore-thought or Contrivance, such as is accompanied with that intense Application of Mind, which we call Study or Premeditation, this indeed is an Imperfection, and such as results from the Limitedness of Understanding, and consequently cannot agree with the Perfection of an infinite Mind. But as for Precogitancy absolutely considered as it signifies only an Ideal View or Preconception of Things, this is so far from being an Imperfection, that without it, God could neither be what he is, nor do what he has done. Nor is it any Objection against Precogitancy in God in this Sense of the Word, to say, that every Thought of the Divine Nature, is immediately Productive, and that to Think with God is to A&t. I grant that Thought in God is immediately Productive in Opposition to any manual Operation, or physical Motion, but not in Opposition to a Fore-thought, which must as Necessity intervene, or mediate. And so again, to Think with God, is the same as to A&t. It is true, in this Sense, that God acts by Thought, and not by Motion. But it is not true, in this Sense, that all Thought in God is Action, and so (which I suppose, is what this Objection means) that there is no Thought in God antecedent to Action, or no Fore-thought. For that Thought whereby God acts, and which is indeed his Action, is Thought of Volition, and in this Sense to Think with God is to A&t; but then there is another Thought in God which regulates and conducts, and in Order of Nature precedes this Action, and that is Thought of Perception or Idea, and in this Sense to Think with God, is Not the Same as to A&t. LETTER IV.

I assure you, my dear Corinna, your Letter was very welcome to me, because it brought with it the good News of your being past those Dangers which your late Illness threatened you with; I hope your present Complaints will vanish as soon as you've recovered a little more Strength, but I fear you will not be perfectly well till Spring, the warm Weather being necessary to compleat your Care; but neither that, nor any other thing, can be of use to you, if you're still negligent of your self, if you still keep irregular Hours; you ought to remember that, there's a regard due to your self, as well as to your nearest Relations, and that you cannot without being guilty of a Crime, be careless of your own Life; 'tis a Gift bestowed on you by God, and as such ought to be valued; when he thinks fit to refume it, you must indeed part with it without a Murmur, but till that time, 'tis certainly your Duty to preserve it with all imaginable Care, and I believe your Grandmother loves you too well to expect such Services from you as may endanger it. I'm obliged to you for the Concern you express for my Health; but that Abstinence you fear may prejudice me, is absolutely necessary. The greatest Part of my time is spent in my Closet; there, I meet with nothing to disturb me, nothing to render me uneasy; I find my Books and my Thoughts to be the

I am grieved to hear, you enjoy so little Health, 'tis a Blessing I heartily with you, and all my other Friends, as being one of the greatest Pleasures of Life; while I had the Happiness of possessing it, I could cheerfully struggle with my Misfortunes, be pleasant in the midst of Storms, and easy without any other Company but my Thoughts and Books; but now my Body sinks under the Pressure, and will not keep Pace with my Mind: Life is what I have for many Years had no Reason to be fond of, and a Grave has appeared to me the happiest and best Asylum; I know 'tis
