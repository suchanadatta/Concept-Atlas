Concept Atlas — Documentation

A scholar's search system for 18th-century British moral philosophy: 129 scanned volumes plus 68 clean-text editions (197 indexed sources, 90,975 passages) · 202 concepts · 1,099 relations · 37 authors.

1 · Searching

Start from the landing page (or the / search box in the header). Any query works — natural language included:

  • “What is love?” — question words are stripped; matches the Love concept and passages about love.
  • “self-love and benevolence” — multi-word AND across pages.
  • “virtue” — archaic spellings (vertue, felf for self, loue for love) are searched automatically.

Two search modes

🧠 semanticJasper embedding model (600M, token-compression) finds passages by meaning, then blends in lexical overlap (0.6 semantic + 0.4 lexical). Every result shows its score: score 0.82 (sem 0.70 · lex 1.00).
⌨ keywordClassic term matching with archaic variants and per-page hit counts (×N). Instant.

Results view

  • Passages tab — click any passage: the book opens in the right-hand reader at that exact page with every matching word boxed on the scan.
  • Concepts tab — matched concepts; click to open the floating concept graph focused on it.
  • Graph tab — the dynamic query graph: your query at the center, document hits and concepts around it, linked by relevance.
  • The search bar at the top of results lets you refine the query without going back.

5 · The floating graph window

From search results, a concept click opens a movable (drag the title bar), resizable (drag the corner) mini-graph over the results. The ⇄ static/dynamic button flips between the full concept map and a graph built live from your search results.

6 · The two query graphs

The Graph tab opens a network for your query. It has three modes:

ConceptThe curated map: the concepts an editor recorded, the documents attesting them, and the relations between them (supports, opposes, …). Only shows ideas that are in the concept map.
SemanticBuilt from the passage embeddings alone. Nodes are the retrieved passages; edges are cosine similarity in four bands (near-identical wording → loosely related). Communities are found by weighted label propagation and named by their distinctive terms. This works for anything the corpus discusses, whether or not the concept map records it.
OverlayBoth at once: the curated concepts drawn inside the passage network, each linked to the passages it actually lands on.

An embedding can only say how close two passages sit; it cannot say one supports another. That is why the semantic graph's edge names describe closeness rather than argument, and why the curated relations only appear in Concept and Overlay mode.

Clicking any node — either graph, 2D or 3D — opens the reader at that page with the passage highlighted on the scan.

The by women / men / joint chips filter whichever graph is on screen. A passage or document node names its book, so the registry answers it outright; a concept has no single book, so it survives if any of its evidence was written by a chosen group. Picking two groups is a union, not an intersection. The toolbar reports how many nodes and links are left, because a filter that removes two thirds of a network should not look like one that removes nothing.

7 · Authors

The Authors button opens the registry: 37 creators with their sex and life dates, joined to every indexed source and to the concept map. It powers the Written by filter in the search rail, and shows what each group of writers spends its pages on — normalised by each group's own attestations, so it is not merely a restatement of who published more. Counts are shown because several are thin; treat them as suggestive.

The same filter sits on the concept map itself, under Written by in the Explore panel. It asks which parts of the map each group of writers actually reaches: of 202 concepts, the women in this corpus attest 99, and 13 of those are attested by no male-authored book here. Choose one group and the panel says how many of its concepts are unique to it.

Every filter joins on book id, not on the author's name. Names are not enough: the corpus holds two Burnets — Elizabeth, and Thomas writing with Locke — and Mary Astell appears both alone and in the joint correspondence with Norris, so a surname alone put 15 of the 202 books in the wrong group. The ambiguous Selected Correspondence was settled by finding its text in the Burnet-and-Locke edition.

8 · Ask the corpus

The 💬 Ask button opens a chat panel that runs against a local model with a 550k-token window. Nothing is retrieved behind your back: it reads only what you attach.

Add to context takes six kinds of thing — a whole book, a page range of one, a concept (which brings its definition, aliases, every relation the map records and the verbatim quotes attesting it), an individual passage, a whole search (the full text of every hit, not a summary), or a free note. The panel shows each item's size in tokens against the window, and each answer names which items it was grounded in. The same action is available where you already are: + context on a search result, +ctx for a whole result set, the 💬 button in the reader (this page, a window around it, or the whole book), and add to chat context on a concept card.

The model's reasoning arrives on its own stream and sits in a collapsed panel above the answer, so you can read how it got there without it cluttering the prose.

It also has tools. Attaching context tells it what to read; the tools let it go looking. Each one's description says when to reach for it, so an answer the first search cannot ground sends it to the map, the relations, or a differently-worded search rather than to a guess. The panel shows every call it makes and what came back, so a wrong answer can be traced to the step that produced it.

search_corpusthe 90,975 passages, in four rankings: hybrid, pure semantic, BM25 and keyword. The first stop for almost anything.
read_sourceany book at any page range, when a snippet will not settle what an author actually claims.
find_conceptthe map, when the idea may be recorded under a name you did not guess.
concept_detaila concept's definition, aliases and relations — one hop, or two, to reach it through a neighbour.
concept_passagesthe quotations attesting a concept.
compare_ideastwo to four ideas side by side: how close they sit in the embedding space, what the map records between them, and the passages where they are argued together rather than separately.
query_networkthe concept-and-document network for a query, to see the territory before picking a direction.
list_bookswhat is on the shelf, filterable by author, by the author's sex and by year.
list_authorsthe registry: dates, output, and what each writer spends their pages on.
verify_quotewhether a wording actually occurs in the corpus, and where. Its useful answer is no.
web_search / web_fetchthe open web — only when the switch is on.

Citations are generated as it reads. Every passage a tool yields is registered for that turn and handed to the model as a marker to quote verbatim — [S3], not a prose reference. Markers that resolve become buttons; clicking one opens that exact book at that exact page with the cited region highlighted, or the text edition scrolled to the passage. A marker the model invents rather than earns stays plain text, so it cannot look like a source that does not exist. If it spends its tool budget still searching, it is made to answer from what it has gathered instead of stopping with nothing said.

It checks its own answer. When the draft is written, the model reads it again with the tools still in hand: it runs verify_quote on every phrase it put in quotation marks, asks whether each marker's passage really supports the claim it is attached to, and looks for assertions carrying no citation at all. Then it either stands by the draft or replaces it. The panel above the answer says which happened, and opens to show the checking — so a corrected answer is visibly a second draft rather than a first one you have to take on trust.

Answers are laid out properly. Headings, lists, blockquotes, code and real tables all render — a table is the right shape for setting four authors against each other on the same axes. A fenced mermaid block is drawn as a diagram, which is worth having when the shape of an argument, or of a set of relations, is the thing being explained. A diagram that will not parse shows its source rather than vanishing.

Web search is a switch, off by default. Turned on, it adds two more tools — a web search and a page fetch — and anything they return is cited as coming from the open web rather than from these 197 sources. Turned off, they are not in the model's tool list at all, so it is a RAG interface over the corpus and nothing else.

Accounts and sessions. Conversations belong to an account, so they survive a reload. Each is stored as a gzipped JSONL file with SQLite as the index. Export downloads the transcript, the reasoning and the full text of every attached context item — so another person can Import it and get the whole conversation, including what it was reading. Forgotten passwords are reset by entering your email; there is no mail server on this host, so the reset code is shown for you to copy.

9 · Debate

⚖ Debate, in the chat's toolbar, sets two agents to argue a motion out of the corpus. You give the motion — a claim that can honestly be argued both ways — and, if you like, a voice for each side ("a reader of Mandeville and Hobbes"). Each speaker has the full toolset and searches for its own evidence; neither is fed a position to recite.

Round one is openings. The last round is closings. Anything in between is rebuttal, where each side has the other's argument in front of it and is required to name the specific claim it is contesting, and to concede what it cannot answer. Then an adjudicator — which has the same tools, and uses them to check rather than to argue — reports where the two actually disagreed, which claims the corpus bears out, what these 197 sources cannot settle, and a verdict on the evidence. It draws the shape of the disagreement as a diagram.

All three share one citation table, so [S7] means the same passage in the opening, the rebuttal and the verdict, and stays clickable throughout. The whole debate is stored as one message in the session, so it exports and imports like any other conversation.

It is slow — several agent turns, each with its own searches — and it is honest about failure: a side that cannot find support for a point is instructed to say so and argue something weaker instead. An adjudication that concludes the corpus does not settle the motion is a legitimate result, not a hedge.

10 · Comparing

⇆ Compare holds two to four things side by side. They can be concepts from the map or any free text, so an idea the map never recorded can still be compared. You get a similarity matrix from the embeddings (the corpus's own answer, not an editor's), the relations the map records between the picked concepts, the strongest passages each idea owns, and shared ground — passages ranked by the weakest of their scores across the items, so a passage that is excellent for one and irrelevant to another cannot get in. That last section is where the ideas are actually argued together.

11 · Under the hood

  • OCR: GLM-OCR (VLM) + docTR word geometry, 300 dpi, validated 0 problems.
  • Concept map: 15 extractors → 20 verifiers → 20 double-auditors → 25 independent auditors (1,096 verdicts); every evidence quote is a verbatim OCR substring.
  • Semantic search: Jasper-Token-Compression-600M, 90,975 passages (OCR + text editions), 2048-dim. Four ranking modes — hybrid (0.6·cosine + 0.4·IDF-weighted lexical), pure semantic (cosine only), BM25 (postings lists, archaic-spelling expanded), and keyword.
  • Query vectors are cached, so switching ranking mode or opening the graph for a query you already ran costs nothing.
  • The engine runs on CPU or GPU; switch it in Settings at the bottom of the page, which also reports index size, device and cache hit rate.
